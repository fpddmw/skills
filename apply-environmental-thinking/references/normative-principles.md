# Normative Principles: Judging Protection and Responsibility

Environmental decisions concern what should be protected, how burdens and
benefits should be distributed, whose knowledge and voice count, and how
uncertainty should affect commitment. Evidence informs these judgments without
determining them by itself.

## Sustainability and Regeneration

Sustainability concerns the conditions that allow valued ecological and social
functions to continue, regenerate, and adapt. It shifts attention from a
momentary output to the capacities, relationships, and option sets passed
forward.

Some losses can be restored or compensated; others transform a unique place,
species, relationship, or future possibility. Judgments about substitution
therefore depend on what is considered commensurable and which minimum
conditions deserve protection.

## Prevention and Precaution

Prevention gives priority to changing the source or generating conditions of
harm. It reflects the insight that downstream control may leave the pressure,
dependency, or burden-producing relation intact.

Precaution addresses consequential uncertainty. Plausible pathways, severity,
recoverability, exposure, learning time, and the reversibility of commitment
shape how much evidence is appropriate before action. Precaution can preserve
the conditions for learning while protecting others from residual risk.

## Responsibility

Responsibility can be grounded in causal contribution, benefit, control,
historical involvement, capacity to act, or an assigned duty of care. These
grounds may point toward different actors and different obligations.

Polluter-pays and extended-responsibility ideas reconnect residual costs with
the activities that generate them. Responsibility also includes monitoring,
disclosure, repair, and stewardship after an initial decision.

## Environmental Justice

Distributional justice concerns who receives benefits, protection, exposure,
loss, or compensation. Procedural justice concerns who can shape, understand,
contest, and appeal a decision. Recognition concerns whose identity,
experience, dependence, and knowledge are treated as relevant.

Equal formal options can produce unequal real freedom. Participation, refusal,
or exit becomes effective through resources, protection, recoverability, and a
viable path back. A burden can migrate toward people with less capacity to
exercise the same nominal choice.

## Future Generations and Shared Worlds

Future people inherit ecological conditions, infrastructure, institutions,
debts, maintenance needs, knowledge, and remaining options. Intergenerational
judgment concerns the quality and flexibility of that inheritance, not only the
discounted size of a future benefit or loss.

Environmental questions also involve more-than-human life, shared resources,
and places whose value is not exhausted by ownership or price. Stewardship,
rights, need, contribution, capability, and protection of a minimum floor offer
different grounds for judgment.

## Participation and Knowledge

Affected people hold situated knowledge about exposure, dependence, feasible
adaptation, and lived consequence. Scientific, technical, local, experiential,
and Indigenous knowledge can reveal different parts of an environmental
system.

Participation changes both legitimacy and understanding. It can alter the
problem definition, evidence considered, alternatives imagined, distribution
of authority, and terms of accountability.

Normative clarity appears when a judgment names the value or responsibility
that supports it and remains distinguishable from the empirical claim on which
it relies.
