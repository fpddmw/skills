# Analytical Models: Explaining Environmental Relationships

Environmental analytical models make coupled relationships observable,
comparable, and open to evidence. They are different representations of a
problem, connected by the causal account rather than assembled as a checklist.

## Defining the System and the Comparison

A boundary determines which places, stages, actors, populations, and time
horizons enter an account. Changing it can reveal that a local reduction is a
transfer of cost, work, risk, or residual burden. The useful boundary keeps
consequential effects within the account and represents their materiality and
evidential strength.

A comparison also needs a counterfactual and a common function. The relevant
question is what would happen otherwise and whether alternatives provide
comparable service, quality, duration, and reliability. Symmetric treatment of
alternatives prevents the incumbent from disappearing outside the analysis.

## Following Transformation and Consequence

Mass and energy balances formalize continuity in physical systems. Material and
energy metabolism connects extraction, transformation, use, circulation,
storage, loss, and release across a wider system.

Stock-flow representation belongs within this family. It is powerful when a
measurable state persists and rates of input, removal, depletion, or
regeneration change that state. Its transferable insight is system memory:
current activity can differ from the condition being accumulated or depleted.
In less material domains, persistence and regeneration carry the insight more
faithfully than an implication of physical conservation.

Fate and transport models follow a source through movement, partitioning,
transformation, degradation, and contact with a receptor. Environmental effect
depends on this pathway as well as the intensity and duration of exposure,
susceptibility, and capacity to recover. A hazard becomes a situated risk
through these relationships.

Life-cycle and industrial-ecology models extend the account across production,
operation, maintenance, replacement, and end of life. Their counterfactual
distinguishes substitution from addition. Leakage describes an avoided burden
reappearing elsewhere; rebound describes efficiency or lower cost increasing
total demand. Circularity concerns the net reduction of virgin input after
collection, processing loss, quality change, and demand growth are included.

## Understanding Dynamics

Feedback turns an effect into a future cause. Reinforcing relations amplify
change; balancing relations constrain it; delay separates action from the
signal that might correct it. A feedback account is a causal hypothesis whose
links have direction, mechanism, timing, and observable implications.

Nonlinearity means the effect of additional pressure depends on system state.
Thresholds and regime shifts describe consequential changes in behavior;
resilience describes how valued functions are maintained, recovered, adapted,
or transformed under disturbance. Diversity, modularity, slack, and
reversibility can preserve response options that average efficiency does not
show.

Regeneration may depend on developmental pathways such as practice, entry,
transmission, succession, and feedback. Removing an apparently low-value stage
can later reduce a system's ability to replenish capability, diversity, or
institutional memory.

## Interpreting Risk, Uncertainty, and Institutions

Risk analysis connects hazard, pathway, exposure, vulnerability, consequence,
and recoverability. Scenario analysis explores how different future conditions
change those relations. Irreversibility concerns a valued state or option set
that cannot be restored within the decision horizon.

Uncertainty changes the confidence and decision value of a claim. Measurement,
model, scenario, and knowledge uncertainty have different implications; the
cost of error can also differ between directions.

Social-ecological and commons models add institutions to the causal system.
Resource users, beneficiaries, exposed groups, decision makers, monitors, and
future inheritors can occupy different positions. Access rules, incentives,
collective action, accountability, and repair shape both environmental pressure
and the capacity to respond.

Let these models organize the causal account by making consequential
relationships observable, explaining how they operate, and showing what
evidence could support or challenge them. Use an environmental model or concept
when it gives the relationship its most exact and illuminating form, then
unfold its mechanism in the language of the target domain. This keeps
disciplinary provenance and causal meaning together.
